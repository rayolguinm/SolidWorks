SECTION C

Engineering analysis

The math behind the viewports and the mechanisms, written up the way an ECE lab report would be. Every number here is computed from the geometry or transforms stored in the submitted files and checked against a closed-form result. Methods and constants are cited in APA 7 style; the full list and a claim-by-claim audit are in Section E.

Why an EE cares. Electronics ship inside mechanical parts: enclosures, heat-sink mounts, connector brackets, harness routing and instrument housings all start as a SolidWorks model like these, and the board has to fit it to a tolerance.
Why a planetary scientist cares. The triangle-mesh math used here (surface normals, divergence-theorem volumes, chord error) is the same math behind digital terrain models of planetary surfaces.
Why a mathematician cares. Viewports are linear maps, assemblies are elements of $SE(3)$, mass properties are surface integrals, and every mechanism is a constraint equation.
C.1

Viewports as linear projections

Studio · HW5 drawings

An orthographic view drops one coordinate. In third-angle projection (ANSI) the Front, Top and Right views are the maps (Carlbom & Paciorek, 1978)

$$P_{F}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad P_{T}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad P_{R}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{u}=P\,\mathbf{x}.$$

The isometric view looks down the body diagonal $\hat{\mathbf{d}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)$: rotate $45^\circ$ about $y$, then tilt by the elevation

$$\alpha=\arcsin\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right)=\arctan\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\approx 35.264^\circ,\qquad R_{\text{iso}}=R_x(\alpha)\,R_y(45^\circ).$$

Each principal axis then projects with the same foreshortening factor $\lVert P R_{\text{iso}}\,\mathbf{e}_i\rVert=\sqrt{2/3}\approx0.8165$, which is why isometric drawings can be measured along all three axes with one scale (Carlbom & Paciorek, 1978). The perspective viewport uses the pinhole model $\mathbf{u}=\tfrac{f}{z_c}(x_c,y_c)$ with vertical field of view $32^\circ$.

C.2

Assemblies as rigid transforms in $SE(3)$

HW7 · HW8 · HW9

Mates solve for one homogeneous transform per component instance. SolidWorks stores it as a row-vector map, which I decoded from the assembly files to place every part in the viewer (homogeneous-transform notation after Denavit & Hartenberg, 1955):

$$\mathbf{p}'=\mathbf{p}\,R+\mathbf{t},\qquad T=\begin{bmatrix}R^{\mathsf T}&\mathbf{t}^{\mathsf T}\\\mathbf{0}^{\mathsf T}&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad R^{\mathsf T}R=I,\ \det R=+1.$$

Example, the brass sliding jaw in the machine vise (HW8, SlidingJaw-1):

$$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{t}=(136.43,\ 1.6,\ 0)\ \text{mm}.$$

That is a $90^\circ$ rotation about $y$ plus a translation along the lead screw. Nested sub-assemblies compose by matrix product, e.g. a handle ball is placed by $T_{\text{vise}\leftarrow\text{handle}}\,T_{\text{handle}\leftarrow\text{ball}}$. Repeated instances (four PG20-1 pegs in Project 5-3) share one tessellation and differ only in $T$.

C.3

Mass properties from the divergence theorem

All parts · Studio info panel

With $\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{F}=1$ for $\mathbf{F}=\tfrac13\mathbf{x}$, Gauss's theorem turns volume into a surface sum over the closed, outward-oriented triangle mesh $\{(\mathbf{v}_0,\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2)_k\}$ (Mirtich, 1996; Zhang & Chen, 2001):

$$V=\iiint_\Omega dV=\frac13\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{x}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS=\frac16\sum_{k}\mathbf{v}_{0}\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{1}\times\mathbf{v}_{2}\right).$$

The same signed tetrahedra give the centroid and the inertia tensor about it (per unit density $\rho$; Tonon, 2005):

$$\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_k V_k\,\frac{\mathbf{v}_0+\mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2}{4},\qquad I_{ij}=\rho\!\iiint_\Omega\!\left(\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2\delta_{ij}-r_ir_j\right)dV,$$

and the principal moments are the eigenvalues of $I$. Surface area is $A=\tfrac12\sum_k\lVert(\mathbf{v}_1-\mathbf{v}_0)\times(\mathbf{v}_2-\mathbf{v}_0)\rVert$. The studio's info panel shows these for every model, with mass $m=\rho V$ where the file or drawing states a material.

C.4

Tessellation and chord error

How the 3D data was recovered

Each B-rep face is stored as triangle strips. A strip of $n$ vertices yields $n-2$ triangles with alternating winding (Evans et al., 1996):

$$\triangle_j=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{v}_j,\mathbf{v}_{j+1},\mathbf{v}_{j+2}) & j\ \text{even}\\(\mathbf{v}_{j+1},\mathbf{v}_j,\mathbf{v}_{j+2}) & j\ \text{odd}\end{cases},\qquad \hat{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{(\mathbf{v}_1-\mathbf{v}_0)\times(\mathbf{v}_2-\mathbf{v}_0)}{\lVert\cdot\rVert}.$$

Winding is checked against the stored vertex normals ($\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf n_{\text{stored}}>0$). A circle of radius $r$ cut into $N$ chords has sagitta (chord height) $\delta=r\left(1-\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\right)$, so meeting a tolerance $\delta$ needs (Piegl & Tiller, 1998)

$$N\ \ge\ \left\lceil\frac{\pi}{\arccos\!\left(1-\delta/r\right)}\right\rceil .$$

Across the portfolio: 178,732 triangles on 9,775 B-rep faces in the part files. The edge overlay draws a crease wherever adjacent normals differ by more than $28^\circ$: $\hat{\mathbf n}_a\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_b<\cos28^\circ$.

C.5

Rack and pinion kinematics

HW8 · Toolbox spur gear 2M 50T 20° PA

Metric involute gearing with module $m=2$ mm and $N=50$ teeth (Litvin & Fuentes, 2004):

$$\begin{aligned} d&=mN=100\ \text{mm}, & d_a&=d+2m=104\ \text{mm},\\ d_b&=d\cos\phi=93.97\ \text{mm}, & p&=\pi m=6.283\ \text{mm}.\end{aligned}$$

The Rack Pinion mate enforces rolling without slip at the pitch line, a linear constraint between pinion angle and rack travel (Litvin & Fuentes, 2004):

$$x=r\,\theta=\tfrac{d}{2}\,\theta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta x_{\text{rev}}=\pi d=314.16\ \text{mm},$$ $$\theta_{\max}=\frac{L_{\text{rack}}}{r}=\frac{250\ \text{mm}}{50\ \text{mm}}=5.00\ \text{rad}\approx 286.5^\circ .$$

Mesh check: tip diameter 103.99 mm (ideal 104), face width 5.00 mm (spec 5), rack length 250 mm (spec 250). Pinion centre to rack tooth tips measures 48.75 mm vs. the ideal $r-m=48$ mm, i.e. the rack sits 0.75 mm further out than tight mesh, which shows up as backlash.

C.6

Four-bar rocker: Grashof and transmission angle

HW7 · Project 5-12 with rotary motor

Pivot locations come from the decoded peg transforms ($xz$-plane, mm): $O_2=(0.0,\ -80.0)$, $A=(30.0,\ -80.0)$, $B=(119.2,\ -92.0)$, $O_4=(80.0,\ 0.0)$. Link lengths:

$$r_1=\lVert O_4-O_2\rVert=113.14,\quad r_2=30.00,\quad r_3=90.00,\quad r_4=100.00\ \text{mm}.$$

Loop closure $\ r_2e^{i\theta_2}+r_3e^{i\theta_3}=r_1e^{i\theta_1}+r_4e^{i\theta_4}$ (Freudenstein, 1955). Grashof's condition (Barker, 1985) with $s,l$ the shortest and longest links:

$$s+l=143.14\ \le\ p+q=190.00\ \checkmark$$

and since the shortest link is the input crank adjacent to ground, the linkage is a crank-rocker: the motor can spin $r_2$ continuously (Barker, 1985). Transmission angle extremes, rocker swing and time ratio:

$$\cos\mu=\frac{r_3^2+r_4^2-r_1^2-r_2^2\pm2r_1r_2}{2r_3r_4}\ \Rightarrow\ \mu\in[51.6^\circ,\ 97.6^\circ]$$ $$\psi=\angle_{O_4}\!\big(r_3{+}r_2\big)-\angle_{O_4}\!\big(r_3{-}r_2\big)=36.3^\circ,\qquad Q=\frac{191.2^\circ}{168.8^\circ}=1.132 .$$

$\mu_{\min}=51.6^\circ$ clears the classic $40^\circ$ guideline for good force transmission (Alt, 1932; Balli & Chand, 2002).

C.7

Extension spring from a swept helix

HW4 · Problem 3-6 (3D sketch + sweep)

The body is a circle of wire diameter $d$ swept along the helix

$$\mathbf{r}(t)=\left(\tfrac{D}{2}\cos t,\ \tfrac{p}{2\pi}t,\ \tfrac{D}{2}\sin t\right),\qquad \tan\lambda=\frac{p}{\pi D}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=3.28^\circ .$$

Measured from the model: $d=2.38$ mm (sketch dimension), $OD=27.78$ mm, so $D=OD-d=25.40$ mm ($\approx1.000$ in), pitch $p=4.572$ mm ($0.180$ in, fitted to the mesh by maximising phase coherence), about $n_a\approx12.5$ active coils. Spring index and rate:

$$C=\frac{D}{d}=10.67,\qquad k=\frac{G\,d^4}{8\,D^3\,n_a}=1.55\ \text{N/mm},$$ $$K_W=\frac{4C-1}{4C-4}+\frac{0.615}{C}=1.135 .$$

Assumes music wire with $G\approx79.3$ GPa ($11.5$ Mpsi), a handbook value that shifts slightly with wire diameter; the part has no material assigned, so $k$ is an estimate. $4\le C\le12$ is the commonly recommended index range (Budynas & Nisbett, 2020). The Wahl factor $K_W$ corrects shear stress $\tau=K_W\frac{8FD}{\pi d^3}$ for wire curvature (Wahl, 1963).

C.8

Threads, callouts and fastener stress area

A callout such as 1/4-20 UNC-1B encodes major diameter $d$, threads per inch $n$ (pitch $P=1/n$) and class of fit. The tensile stress area used for bolt sizing (ASME, 2019; ISO, 2013):

$$A_t=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(d-\frac{0.9743}{n}\right)^{2}\ \ \text{(UN)},\qquad A_t=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(d-0.9382P\right)^{2}\ \ \text{(metric)}.$$

C.9

Design for 3D printing: the LunaREX desk sculpture

Project 1 · STL export and print analysis

An STL is a bare list of triangles. A binary STL stores an 80-byte header, a 4-byte count and 50 bytes per facet (normal, three vertices, attribute), so the exported file size is fixed by the facet count (Szilvási-Nagy & Mátyási, 2003):

$$S=84+50F=84+50\,(39{,}952)=1{,}997{,}684\ \text{bytes}\ \checkmark$$

A slicer needs a watertight 2-manifold: every edge shared by exactly two facets (Szilvási-Nagy & Mátyási, 2003). After welding coincident vertices the export has 0 boundary edges and 0 non-manifold edges in 1 shell. For a closed triangle mesh $2E=3F$, and Euler's formula gives the topology:

$$\chi=V-E+F=19{,}978-59{,}928+39{,}952=2\ \Rightarrow\ g=\tfrac{2-\chi}{2}=0,\qquad F=2V-4\ \checkmark$$

Volume, area and centroid follow from the divergence-theorem sums in C.3:

$$V=40{,}520.9\ \text{mm}^3,\qquad A=18{,}942.2\ \text{mm}^2,\qquad \bar{\mathbf x}=(35.93,\ 20.64,\ 19.30)\ \text{mm}.$$

Printed upright on the drafted base, the slicer stacks

$$N=\left\lceil\frac{h}{\Delta z}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{71.30}{0.20}\right\rceil=357\ \text{layers}.$$

Using the common $45^\circ$ rule of thumb, a facet is flagged when it faces down by more than $45^\circ$ from vertical, $\hat{n}_y<-\cos45^\circ$, excluding the bed contact. The real printable threshold varies with fan speed, print speed and temperature (Jiang, Stringer, et al., 2018; Jiang, Xu, & Stringer, 2018). That totals $A_{\text{oh}}=853.5$ mm², only 4.5% of the surface, so the part needs little support. These are the downward faces of the relief (for example the undersides of the raised letters).

PLA mass, solid versus a typical slicer setting of shell thickness $t=0.8$ mm and infill fraction $\varphi=0.15$:

$$m_{\text{solid}}=\rho V=50.2\ \text{g},\qquad m\approx\rho\left[A t+\varphi\,(V-At)\right]\approx 23.5\ \text{g}\quad(\rho=1.24\ \text{g/cm}^3).$$

PLA density $1.21$ to $1.25$ g/cm³ (Farah et al., 2016). The shell plus infill expression is my own first-order estimate, not a slicer result: it treats the shell as $A\,t$ and ignores top and bottom layers and infill pattern.

The 3° draft on the base walls and the R6.4 corner fillets are read from the drawing; their effect on print quality was not measured here.
